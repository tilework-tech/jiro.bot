## Techniques

- **Storytelling:** it opens on *place and people*, not product. A dark, cinematic "arrival at the studio" leads to hands-on craft (3D printing, spec sheets, physical characters), then human delight (a smile, a touch), then the agent "coming alive" on screens (rings morph into characters). Only after about 15 s does it show the actual software UI, and even then as a physical device on a desk. The product is personified as a collectible character (a "dot") before it is ever shown as UI.
- **Pacing:** about 1 to 1.5 s per shot in the opening montage (0 to 6 s). That is fast and rhythmic, almost a cut every 2 frames at 0.5 s sampling. It slows to 2 s for the emotional beat (6 to 8 s), then about 5 s of continuous tracking for the reveal (8 to 13.5 s). Then it goes reaction, UI, reaction, UI at about 1 to 1.5 s each. Fast cuts for texture, long takes for the payoff.
- **Transitions:** all hard cuts. No wipes or fades in this range. Motion inside the frame does the work: slow push-ins, tracking, and print-head movement. The one "transition-like" effect is *in-world*: screen graphics morph from ring to pill to character.
- **Shot vocabulary:** wide establishing, shallow-DOF profile, macro, flat-lay graphic board, product still life, and over-the-shoulder close-ups. Heavy bokeh and practical lighting (lamps, lightboxes) give a premium, film-shot (not motion-graphics) feel.
- **Color:** a dark, warm wood-and-amber base with saturated accent primaries (cyan or blue, magenta, yellow, lime green) carried through *everything*: lightboxes, printed objects, characters, and blurred foregrounds. The brand palette is the set dressing. The spec board is the one bright, white beat, and it acts as a palate cleanser.
- **Product UI framing:** no browser frame and no screen recording. The UI is shown on a physical bezel-framed device, photographed top-down on wood with a prop (headphones), then from an over-the-shoulder angle. The UI is very minimal: large type, about 5 menu items, generous spacing. One deliberate UI motion (`Your dot` inserting at the top, then highlighting) tells you exactly what the new feature is and where it lives. No cursor inside the film.
- **Typography:** no burned-in captions or titles in the film within this range. Explanation lives in the surrounding article text (a bold lede, then plain body copy). The in-UI type is large, light, and grotesk-style (`ChatGPT`, menu items), and it reads clearly even at 60% scale.
- **Narration/audio:** the browser shows a playing-audio speaker in the tab or URL bar, so there is likely music or VO, but none is visible as text. The human reactions (smiles, looking up, speaking to the phone) imply dialogue with the agent.
- **"What am I looking at" clarity:** given by context. The page copy tells you "always-on agents" and the film shows rather than tells. The UI beat names the feature (`Your dot`) with a highlight.
- **Premium cues:** real cinematography (shallow DOF, practical lighting, slow dolly), physical craft objects, a consistent brand-color system across props, restrained UI, and no on-screen text clutter. Note that the player controls and the cursor in the recording are artifacts of the capture, not design choices.
- **Takeaways for Jiro:** (1) Personify the agent as a character (Jiro, the sushi robot) before showing UI. (2) Use a fast 1 s texture montage, then one longer "comes alive" take. (3) Show the UI sparingly, large, and framed as an object, with a single animated highlight on the key feature. (4) Carry a tight accent palette through every shot. (5) Keep text off the video and let the page headline do the explaining.
